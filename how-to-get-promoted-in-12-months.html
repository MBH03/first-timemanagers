How to get promoted to manager in the next 12 months.

If you want to become a manager, there's a good chance you've already heard the standard advice: work hard, take initiative, be a leader, ask your boss for more responsibility. All of that is true. It's also incomplete.

There's another part of getting promoted that people don't like to talk about. Timing matters. Open positions matter. Your manager matters. The size of your company matters. Who knows you matters. And sometimes, despite doing everything right, there simply isn't a management job available for you.

That doesn't mean you should give up. It means you should stop treating a promotion like a reward for working hard, and start treating it like a career project. If I wanted to become a manager within the next 12 months, this is how I'd approach it.

Months 1 and 2: Find Out What You're Actually Working Toward

The first thing I'd do is have a conversation with my manager. Not "can I be promoted?" I'd ask: "I want to work toward becoming a manager. If a management opportunity opened up, what would you need to see from me to feel confident putting me in that role?" Then I'd listen carefully.

You're looking for specifics. Maybe your manager says you need more experience leading projects. Maybe they want to see you handle conflict. Maybe you need to improve your communication. Maybe you need more exposure to other teams. Maybe they think you're ready but there's no opening. Those are very different situations, and you need to know which one you're dealing with. Write down exactly what your manager says, that becomes the scorecard for the next 12 months.

Then ask one more question: "Do you realistically see an opportunity for me to move into management here within the next 12 months?" The answer may be uncomfortable. That's exactly why you should ask. If your manager says yes, you have a path. If they say maybe, you need to understand what has to happen. If they say no, you need to understand whether the problem is your readiness or simply the lack of an opening. And if they can't give you a clear answer, that's information too.

Months 1 and 2: Become the Person People Go To

You don't need direct reports to become useful as a leader. You need people to start relying on you. Become the person who knows how things work. When someone new joins the team, help them get up to speed. When someone is stuck, help them work through the problem. When a process is confusing, clarify it. When something falls through the cracks, help make sure it doesn't happen again. When your boss asks "who can take this," be someone they think of.

But there's an important distinction here. Don't become the person who does everyone's work. Become the person who helps other people do their work better. That's a much more valuable skill, and it's the beginning of what might eventually become your management reputation.

Months 2 and 3: Find a Project That Involves Other People

This is where you need to get practical. Find something that requires coordination. It doesn't need to be a huge project. Maybe there's a process that needs to be fixed. Maybe a new employee needs to be trained. Maybe another team needs help with a recurring problem. Maybe your company is launching something and someone needs to coordinate the work. Raise your hand.

You're looking for opportunities where you have to set a goal, organize people, clarify responsibilities, follow up, communicate, deal with problems, make decisions, and get people moving in the same direction. That's management practice, and it's much better evidence than telling your boss you're a natural leader.

Months 3 and 4: Work With People Outside Your Team

This is one of the most overlooked steps. If you spend your entire career working only with your immediate team, your reputation can become very narrow. Your boss may know you're good. Your teammates may know you're good. But nobody else does. Try to change that.

Look for a project involving another department, sales, operations, finance, product, customer success, engineering, whatever makes sense at your company. You're not doing this because you need more friends. You're doing it because managers work across organizations, and you need to demonstrate that you can work with different personalities, priorities and departments. It also gives you something else that matters later: people who can speak positively about your work outside your immediate team. When your promotion eventually comes up, that matters.

Months 3 and 4: Start Managing Without the Title

This is where the mindset changes. Stop asking yourself "what can I accomplish?" Start asking "what can I help this group accomplish?" That could mean mentoring someone, running a meeting, organizing a project, helping resolve a disagreement, creating a better process, helping a struggling teammate, or taking responsibility for a recurring problem. You're trying to demonstrate that you can create results through other people. That's the job you're trying to get, so demonstrate it before you have the title.

Months 4 and 5: Build One Undeniable Win

You don't need twenty accomplishments. You need a few good ones. I'd specifically look for one project where you can point to a clear before and after: reduce a process from five days to three, improve a customer metric, eliminate a recurring error, save the team hours every week, or take a project nobody owns and get it across the finish line.

The exact accomplishment doesn't matter as much as the pattern: there was a problem, you took ownership, you involved other people, you made decisions, something improved. That's your management story. Keep track of it, don't trust yourself to remember six months later.

Months 5 and 6: Ask for Feedback Before Review Season

Don't wait for your annual review to find out whether your boss thinks you're ready. Ask now.

"I have been working toward management and have taken on more leadership responsibility. Based on what you have seen so far, what am I doing well and what is the biggest thing I still need to improve?"

Then listen. Don't argue. If your manager says you need to communicate better, ask for an example. If they say you need to be more strategic, ask what that would look like. If they say you need more leadership experience, ask what opportunity would give you that experience. You want feedback you can act on.

Month 6: Have the Halfway Conversation

At six months, I'd have another direct conversation with my manager. Bring the scorecard from the first conversation.

"When we talked six months ago, you said I needed to demonstrate X, Y and Z. Since then, I have done these things. How do you think I am progressing? Am I on track to be considered for a management opportunity?"

This is an important moment. You're not asking for a promotion, you're asking whether the plan is working. If the answer is yes, keep going. If the answer is no, you still have six months to change course. If the answer is "you're doing well, but there's no opening," you've learned something very important: you may be ready, the company may simply not have a seat for you.

Months 6 to 8: Become More Visible

This doesn't mean bragging. It means making sure the right people understand what you're doing. Send useful updates. Share results. Give credit to the people who helped. Explain the business impact. If you completed a project, don't just say "project completed." Say:

"We completed the project two weeks ahead of schedule. The new process should eliminate the manual reconciliation we were doing each month. Thanks to Sarah and David for helping with the implementation."

That's visibility without self-promotion. You're showing that you can communicate like a leader, and your boss isn't the only person who should know what you can do.

Months 6 to 8: Build Relationships With Other Leaders

This doesn't mean going around your boss. It means expanding your professional network inside the company. If you work closely with another department leader, get to know them. Ask about their priorities. Understand how your team affects theirs. Ask for feedback after working together. If you have a skip-level opportunity, use it well, don't make every conversation about your promotion. Talk about the business. Ask good questions. Demonstrate that you understand more than your own job.

Over time, you want other leaders to think "that person could probably run a team." That's much stronger than having your boss be the only person making that argument.

Months 7 and 8: Start Operating at the Next Level

There's a phrase you'll hear often: "act like the manager before you become the manager." There's some truth to it, but don't take it too far. You are not the boss. Don't start ordering your coworkers around, don't make decisions you don't have authority to make, don't take over other people's responsibilities.

Instead, demonstrate the behaviors: think about the whole team, bring solutions, help people develop, handle difficult situations calmly, make decisions when you have the authority, communicate clearly, take responsibility, share credit, think about what's best for the business, not just your own workload. Your goal is to make the promotion feel like a logical next step.

Months 8 and 9: Build Your Promotion Case

At this point, you should have evidence. Write it down. You don't need a fancy presentation, just a simple document with your current responsibilities, projects you led, people you trained or mentored, cross-functional work, problems you solved, measurable results, feedback you received, leadership skills you've developed, and examples of handling difficult situations.

Then answer one question: "why am I ready to manage people?" Your answer shouldn't be "because I have worked here for three years." It should be "because I have demonstrated that I can lead work, develop people, solve problems, communicate across teams and deliver results through others." That's a promotion case.

Months 9 and 10: Find Out When the Decision Actually Happens

This is another uncomfortable part of career advancement. You need to understand your company's timing. When are promotions discussed? When are budgets set? When are headcount plans approved? Who actually approves management positions? When does your manager need to make the case?

If you wait until the job is posted, you may already be late. Many promotion decisions are influenced well before the formal announcement. You want to be part of the conversation before the decision is final.

"When does the organization typically make decisions about management openings and promotions? I want to make sure I am working toward the right timeline."

That's not being political. That's understanding how your company works.

Months 10 and 11: Ask the Direct Question

Now you should be direct.

"I have been working toward a management role for the past year. Based on the responsibilities I have taken on and the results I have delivered, do you believe I am ready for a management position?"

Then stop talking. Let your manager answer. If the answer is yes, ask "what needs to happen for me to be considered for the next opening?" If the answer is not yet, ask "what specifically is missing?" If the answer is vague, ask for examples. If the answer is no because there's no opening, that's different, and you need to treat it differently.

Month 12: Make a Decision About the Company

This may be the most important step in the entire plan. Sometimes you're ready. The company knows you're ready. Your manager supports you. And there's still no job. Maybe the current managers aren't leaving. Maybe the company isn't growing. Maybe management layers are being reduced. Maybe your department is simply too small. You cannot work your way out of a structural problem.

At that point, you have a decision: wait, or look elsewhere. There's nothing wrong with leaving your company to get your first management opportunity, in fact, sometimes it's the most realistic path. A company may continue to see you as "the person who does X." Another company may look at your leadership experience and say "we need someone to manage this team." That can be the difference.

Don't stay somewhere indefinitely because you believe you've earned a promotion that the organization doesn't have the structure to provide. Before you leave, though, make sure you've actually built the experience, leading projects, mentoring people, coordinating across teams and owning meaningful outcomes will make the external move much easier.

The 12-Month Plan

If I were putting this into a simple checklist, it would look like this.

  1. Month 1: Tell your manager you want to become a manager.
  2. Month 1: Ask what they would need to see from you.
  3. Month 1: Find out whether a realistic management opening could exist within 12 months.
  4. Month 2: Become the person people go to for help.
  5. Month 2: Volunteer for a leadership opportunity.
  6. Month 3: Lead a project involving other people.
  7. Month 3: Start building relationships outside your team.
  8. Month 4: Take on cross-functional work.
  9. Month 4: Practice leading without formal authority.
  10. Month 5: Deliver a measurable win.
  11. Month 5: Document your results and leadership examples.
  12. Month 6: Ask for honest feedback.
  13. Month 6: Have a formal halfway career conversation.
  14. Month 7: Increase your visibility through useful communication.
  15. Month 7: Build relationships with other leaders.
  16. Month 8: Start demonstrating next-level behaviors.
  17. Month 9: Build your promotion case.
  18. Month 9: Learn how your company's promotion and headcount process works.
  19. Month 10: Confirm the timeline for the next management opening.
  20. Month 11: Ask directly whether your manager believes you are ready.
  21. Month 12: Pursue the internal opportunity or start looking externally.
And Here's the Part Nobody Wants to Hear

You can do everything on this list and still not become a manager in 12 months. That doesn't necessarily mean you failed. Your company may not have an opening. Someone else may be ahead of you. The business may have a hiring freeze. Your manager may leave. The organization may restructure. Or you may discover that management isn't actually what you want.

The goal of the 12-month plan isn't to manufacture a promotion. It's to put yourself in the strongest possible position. At the end of the year, you should know three things: am I ready, does my company have an opportunity for me, and if not, am I ready to find that opportunity somewhere else? That's a much better position than sitting at your desk for another three years hoping someone eventually notices you.

Because getting promoted into management isn't just about working hard. It's about becoming ready, making that readiness visible, understanding the timing, and being willing to move when the opportunity isn't there. Sometimes the fastest way to become a manager is to become the obvious choice where you are. Sometimes it's to become the obvious choice somewhere else. You need to be prepared for both.
