Stage 01 · Week 1

Getting Oriented

Your first week isn't about proving anything. It's about paying attention. What to actually do in your first five days, what to deliberately not touch yet, and how to introduce yourself in a way that earns trust instead of just announcing a title.

01 Listen before you diagnose Spend the week in 1:1s and observation, not decisions. Resist the urge to fix anything in week one, you don't have enough context yet to know if it's actually broken. Read → 02 Meet every person on your team 1:1, not as a group Ask what's working, what's not, and what they wish someone had told them when they started. You'll hear things in a 1:1 that never surface in a team meeting. Read → 03 Learn the actual workflow, not the org-chart version Sit in on a real piece of work getting done and watch where it gets stuck. The org chart tells you who's involved. It doesn't tell you where the friction actually is. Read → 04 Say what you don't know yet, out loud Naming that you're still learning builds more trust than pretending you already have it figured out. Your team already knows you're new, pretending otherwise just costs you credibility. Read →
Team Introduction

Whether you're introducing yourself in a team meeting or one on one, keep it short and let curiosity do the rest of the work.

Opening

"I want to keep this short, you'll learn more about how I work over the next few weeks than from anything I say today. A few things up front: here's what I'm here to do, here's how I like to work, and here's what I'm still learning about this team."

A long opening speech reads as nerves or overcompensation. Say less than feels natural, the team will fill in the rest through how you actually show up.

What I'm here to do

"My job right now is to understand how this team actually works before I change anything. I'm not walking in with a list of fixes."

This single line does more to lower a team's guard than anything else you could say in week one.

How I like to work

"[One or two honest things about your style, e.g. I'd rather you push back in the moment than agree and quietly disagree later. I default to written updates over status meetings.]"

Give them something concrete to calibrate against, not a values statement. "I care about transparency" tells them nothing actionable. "I'll always tell you the real reason behind a decision" does.

Close by handing it back to them

"I'll be setting up 1:1s with each of you this week, that's where I actually want to hear from you, not just today."

Ends the introduction as an opening move, not a finished statement. The real introduction happens over the next several 1:1s, not in this one meeting.
