AI isn't psychic. Stop talking to it like it is.

It's Tuesday night, and you're bargaining with a chatbot like it owes you money. "Give me ideas to motivate my team," you type, then wait, as if the universe's most powerful language model is going to squint through the screen, somehow clock that you're a stressed sales manager in Ohio, intuit that your team just missed Q2, and hand you the exact pep talk that fixes everything.

It does not do that. It was never going to do that. It hands you: recognize good work, set clear goals, build team spirit. The startup-poster version of advice. You could've gotten the same three bullet points from a fortune cookie, and honestly the fortune cookie would've been faster.

Here's the part that stings a little: that's not the AI failing you. That's you, handing a stranger a napkin with "help" written on it and expecting a business plan back.

Brief It Like You'd Brief a New Hire

Think about what happens when you hand an actual new hire a vague task. "Help the team feel motivated." They'd stare at you, blink twice, then ask ten follow-up questions before doing anything useful. What team? What's actually wrong? What does "better" even look like? They're not being difficult. They just know they can't read your mind, because nobody can.

AI has the same problem, except it won't ask the follow ups. It'll just guess. And left to guess, it guesses generic, every time. So the job is yours: hand over the context up front, the way you would with a smart stranger who's never met your team and has zero idea what your Tuesday has looked like.

Watch what happens when you actually do that:

Weak

"Give me ideas to motivate my team."

Strong

"I manage a 12-person remote sales team struggling with low morale after missing Q2 targets. Suggest five creative strategies to boost motivation during our weekly virtual meetings, including tools we can use."

Same question, technically. But now it has a team, a problem, and a shape for the answer to take. Notice the second one doesn't just sound better, it's answerable in a way the first one never was.

Treat the First Answer Like a Draft, Not a Verdict

Here's where most people stop, and it's the expensive mistake. You get an answer, it's fine, you close the tab.

Don't. The first answer is a draft. Treat the conversation like you're actually talking to someone, not filing a request into a box and waiting for output. Push back. Ask it to go deeper. Tell it what's still missing.

A few ways to do that, worth actually having in your back pocket:

When the answer's too broad, say so and ask why
"That answer was too broad, how should I rephrase the question to get something more specific?" You're not just getting a better answer this time, you're getting taught how to ask next time. It's the closest thing to having a mentor who happens to know exactly how prompting works.
Ask it to grade you
"Rate my prompt 1 to 10, and tell me how to make it better." Sounds almost silly the first time you try it. Do it anyway. It turns every question you ask into a small lesson, and after a few rounds you'll notice your first attempts getting sharper without even trying.
Never let a decent answer be the last word
A decent answer is rarely the end of the conversation, it's the middle. Ask: "Can you give real-world examples of how this worked?" Or: "Give me a step-by-step plan to actually do this." The gap between a forgettable answer and one you'd actually use is often just one more question you didn't bother asking.

Some of This Still Has to Be Yours

None of this means outsourcing all your thinking. Some of it should still be yours, done the old-fashioned way, no tool involved. Not because AI can't help, but because your own instincts go soft if you never use them, and honestly, your best ideas will still come from you half the time anyway. Use the tool to sharpen your thinking, not replace the part where you actually think.

The Cheat Sheet

Once this clicks, you don't need to remember all of it, just the shape. Here's the fuller version, sorted by the kind of Tuesday you're having.

Idea generation: "What are [number] creative ways to [goal] for a [team type] team dealing with [challenge]? Include real-world examples."

Coaching: "I asked [your prompt]. Rate it 1 to 10 and suggest how to improve it for more specific, actionable answers."

Follow-up: "Can you provide examples or a step-by-step plan for how [suggestion] has worked for other managers, with measurable results?"

Difficult conversations: "I need to give feedback to a [role] on my team about [specific behavior], and it hasn't landed the last two times I've raised it. Write me an opening line that's direct but not harsh, plus two ways they might respond and how to handle each."

Meeting prep: "I have a [length] one on one with a [role] who's [context, e.g. 'newly promoted and struggling with delegation']. Give me three questions that'll get past status updates and into what's actually going on."

Decision pressure test: "I'm leaning toward [decision] because [reasoning]. Play devil's advocate. What's the strongest case against this, and what am I likely not seeing?"

Writing help: "Rewrite this message to my team about [topic] so it's direct without sounding cold. Here's my draft: [paste it]."

Skill building: "I'm weak at [specific skill, e.g. 'giving negative feedback without softening it into nothing']. Give me a scenario to practice on, then tell me what I got wrong."

Next Tuesday night, when you're staring at that same blinking cursor, skip the napkin. Brief it like you mean it, push back on what comes back, and walk into Monday's all-hands with something your team's never heard before, because it was never generic in the first place.
