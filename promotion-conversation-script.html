Promotion Conversation Script

Good news conversations feel easy from the outside, but they're easy to mess up too. Ramble through the windup, bury the reason under vague praise, or forget to spell out what actually changes, and a moment that should feel like a win ends up feeling confusing, or worse, like it has to be proven before it's real.

The fix isn't more enthusiasm. It's the same thing hard conversations need: know what you're going to say, in what order, before you walk in. Use this as a starting point, not something to read verbatim. Say it in your own words, but keep the order and the directness.

1. Open With the News, Not the Buildup

"I wanted to tell you directly, you're being promoted to [title], effective [date]."

Lead with the news itself. A long windup, "so I wanted to grab some time to talk about your year, and there's been a lot going on, and the team's been noticing," makes good news feel like it's being delayed, and people can tell something's coming before you say it, which makes the wait worse, not better. Say it in the first sentence.

2. Say Why, Specifically

"Here's exactly what you did that earned this: [specific accomplishments]."

Specific reasons make it feel earned. Vague praise makes it feel arbitrary, or worse, political. "You've had a great year" could mean anything. "You took over the client escalation process in Q2 and cut response time in half, and you did it while onboarding two new hires" is something they can point back to on a hard day and know exactly why they're in the room. Have two or three of these ready before you walk in. Don't improvise them live.

3. Name What Changes

"Here's what's different starting now: [scope, reports, expectations]."

Don't leave them to guess what the new role actually involves. New title, new scope, new reports, new expectations, say it plainly. Ambiguity here is what turns a good moment into a confusing first few weeks, where they're not sure what's actually theirs to decide anymore.

4. Close by Making It Theirs

"This is yours because of the work you already did, not a trial."

Removes the unspoken "prove it" that can undercut an otherwise good moment. People will supply their own doubt if you don't close it off, wondering if this is conditional, if there's a probation period nobody mentioned, if they need to spend the next quarter proving it was the right call. Say directly that it isn't. They already earned it. The promotion is the proof, not the test.

Prep Template: Fill This Out Before You Walk In

Title and effective date: ___
Two or three specific accomplishments that earned this: 1. ___ 2. ___ 3. ___
What's changing (scope, reports, expectations): ___
What's staying the same (worth naming, so it's not all unknowns): ___
One thing they might ask that you should have an answer ready for (comp timing, when the team will know, what happens to their old responsibilities): ___

If any of these are still blank, the conversation isn't ready yet either. A promotion delivered with real specifics takes five extra minutes to prepare and lands completely differently than one delivered off the cuff.
