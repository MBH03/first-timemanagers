Workplace Skills · Meetings

How to speak up in a meeting, and get it right four different ways.

Staying quiet too often makes you invisible. Talking too often makes you background noise. Both cost you the same thing: nobody actually listens when you finally say something that matters.

If you've spent years as an individual contributor, the instinct to hang back until you're sure you're right is understandable. But waiting for certainty means you often speak last, after the conversation has already settled, which makes your contribution look like a footnote instead of an input that shaped anything.

Time It, Don't Volume It

The fix is timing, not volume. Time your contribution to when it can still change the outcome, not after the group has already converged. If a decision is forming and you have a concern, that's the moment, not five minutes later once everyone's already nodding along.

Example

"Before we lock this in, I think we're underestimating how long the migration piece will take. Last time something similar took us three weeks, not one. Worth double-checking before we commit to the date publicly?"

A short flag, one piece of evidence, and a question that invites the room to check the assumption, rather than a flat declaration that you're right. That gets your point taken seriously without turning it into a confrontation.

For the opposite problem, say your point once, in two or three sentences, and stop. A longer version rarely fixes a point that didn't land the first time, it usually just makes people tune out sooner next time you speak. Build on what someone just said instead of starting a new thread, "adding to what Sam said" signals you were actually listening, which builds more credibility than a well-formed independent point.

If you're naturally quiet in groups, decide before the meeting on the one thing you actually want to say, and commit to saying it in the first half, before the conversation moves past the point where it's relevant. Waiting for a perfect opening usually means the opening never comes.

Ask the Question That Changes the Outcome

A sharp comment shows you followed along. A sharp question can change the outcome, and it's remembered longer, because it's the thing the room ends up discussing.

Most meeting questions fall into one of two weak categories: the clarifying question the doc already answered, or the "devil's advocate" question that isn't really trying to get anywhere, just signaling engagement. The ones people remember surface a real gap, an assumption nobody's tested, a risk nobody's named.

Example: weak

"Just to clarify, when you say Q3, do you mean calendar Q3 or fiscal Q3?"

Example: strong

"This plan assumes the vendor integration is done by Q3, what happens to the rest of the timeline if that slips, and do we have a fallback if it does?"

The strong version names an assumption everyone in the room was quietly relying on, asks about a consequence that actually matters, and invites the room to solve something rather than clarify a detail. As you listen, track what's being assumed rather than what's being stated outright, that's where the next good question is hiding.

The best question in the room is the one nobody else was going to ask, the one everyone needed answered, not necessarily the hardest one on the table.

Tone matters as much as content. "Did anyone actually think about X?" reads as an attack and gets a defensive answer. "I might be missing something, but how does this account for X?" gets an honest one, and makes you the person asked to weigh in next time.

Interrupt Like You're Serving the Room

Not interrupting isn't always the polite choice. Sometimes it just means a meeting runs long, or the same point gets repeated a third time because nobody redirected it. The skill is interrupting for the right reason, in a way that's clearly in service of the group, not a grab for airtime.

Example

"Sorry to jump in, I want to make sure we don't lose the thread on the budget question before we move on, since we're at time."

A brief acknowledgment, "sorry to jump in," signals you're aware you're interrupting and doing it deliberately, not carelessly talking over someone. Skip that signal, even with something useful to add, and it tends to land as inconsiderate regardless of the content.

One category worth naming on its own: redirecting a meeting that's run long or gone off-topic. It's one of the most valuable interruptions a first-time manager can learn, and one of the most avoided, because redirecting a conversation you're not formally running can feel presumptuous.

Example

"We've got ten minutes left and haven't covered the budget question yet, should we come back to this later?"

Neutral and specific. Nobody feels overridden.

When You're the One Who Gets Cut Off

The instinct in the moment is to let it go and pick the thread back up later, or never. Do that enough times and the pattern doesn't fix itself, it becomes the room's default expectation of how meetings with that person work.

The in-the-moment response is calm and short. A level "let me finish this point" or "one more second" holds your ground without making the moment bigger than it needs to be. Said evenly, it reads as confident, not combative.

Example: in the moment

"Jordan, let me just finish this thought, then I want to hear your take." Said calmly, mid-sentence, then you actually finish the thought before yielding the floor.

A one-time interruption is usually just enthusiasm or bad timing, not worth addressing directly. A repeated pattern with the same person is worth a direct, private conversation, separate from any specific meeting.

Addressing a pattern in the moment makes it about that meeting. Addressing it privately makes it about the pattern, which is the actual problem.

Example: the private conversation

"I've noticed in a few of our meetings I've gotten cut off mid-point pretty often. I don't think it's intentional, but I wanted to flag it because I want to make sure my input's actually landing. Would you be open to me finishing my thought before you jump in?"

Framing it as wanting your input to land, rather than as an accusation, gets a much better reception. Most people genuinely don't realize they're doing it.

If you notice someone else getting repeatedly interrupted in a meeting you're part of, a simple "I don't think they finished" on their behalf is one of the more quietly useful things you can do in a room, for them, and for how the room sees you.

Pick the one of these four you're worst at, and use it in your next meeting.
