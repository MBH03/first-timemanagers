Becoming a Manager

Get comfortable with accountability.

A lot of people want the authority of management without the accountability. They want credit for the decision, not the blame for how it turned out. Management doesn't work that way.

If your team misses a deadline, you can't just blame the employee who was assigned the task. You need to ask whether the expectations were clear, whether the resources were available, whether the timeline was realistic and whether you followed up appropriately. None of that excuses poor performance. But it's on you to understand your own role in the conditions that produced it, not just the employee's.

Start practicing this now. When something goes wrong, explain what happened without exaggerating or hiding your part in it. Then describe what you'll do differently next time. A simple statement such as, "I didn't clarify the handoff, so the team made different assumptions. I'll add a checkpoint before the next phase," demonstrates maturity. People trust leaders who can acknowledge reality.

Accountability is especially important when the outcome isn't entirely under your control. You may not have caused the problem, but you still have a responsibility to respond to it. Finding someone to blame quickly feels satisfying. It doesn't fix anything. The real job is understanding what happened, protecting the team from unnecessary damage, and improving the system underneath it.

When discussing a mistake, separate four things:

  1. The facts
  2. The contributing conditions
  3. Your responsibility
  4. The corrective action

This prevents two common errors. The first is defensiveness, where you explain why nothing was your fault. The second is excessive self-blame, where you accept responsibility for factors you couldn't control. Good accountability is accurate, specific and forward-looking.

Owning It Out Loud, in the Room

"That's on me. I didn't build in enough buffer for the data to come in late, and I should have. Here's what I'm doing about it, and here's what changes next time."

Example Accountability Statement

"The report was delivered one day late because the final data was not available when the draft was prepared. I should have confirmed the data deadline earlier and created a backup plan. I have contacted the stakeholder and sent an updated timeline. For future reports, I will confirm dependencies at the start and add a checkpoint two days before delivery."

Accountability Mistakes to Avoid
  • Blaming one person before understanding the process
  • Hiding bad news until the problem becomes larger
  • Apologizing without explaining what will change
  • Accepting responsibility for things outside your control

Tip: accountability is not self-blame. Being honest about your role isn't the same as absorbing blame for everything that went wrong. Separate facts from excuses, then focus on what you'll do differently.

Fun fact: nobody actually trusts the manager who's never wrong. Admitting the miss is usually what makes people willing to bring you the next one early, while it's still small.
