Home / Workplace Skills / Busy vs. Valuable
Workplace Skills · Judgment

A full calendar isn't a result. It's just a full calendar.

"I've been slammed" isn't the flex it feels like when you say it. It's often a sign that your time and your priorities have quietly come apart from each other.

Busyness is easy to mistake for value because it's visible in a way results often aren't. A packed calendar, a full inbox, and back-to-back meetings all look like proof of importance from the outside. But none of it actually answers the question that matters, which is whether the right things moved forward. It's entirely possible to be completely underwater and have almost nothing to show for the week, because the hours went to whatever was loudest, not whatever mattered most.

The habit worth building is a short, honest check at the end of each week: what actually moved because of what I did. Not what I attended. Not what I responded to. What moved. If the answer is thin despite a packed week, that's a signal worth taking seriously, not as a character flaw, but as a sign your time and your priorities have drifted apart.

Example

A manager spends a week in eleven meetings, answers ninety Slack messages, and clears their inbox twice a day. At the end of the week, the one project actually due to leadership hasn't moved, because none of that activity was actually it. Busy, genuinely, exhaustingly busy, and not valuable, in the specific sense that matters.

This isn't an argument for doing less. It's an argument for being deliberate about which busy you choose. Two people can work the same number of hours in a week and produce completely different amounts of real progress, because one of them let their calendar get filled by other people's priorities and the other one protected time for their own.

A practical fix: before agreeing to a new meeting or task, ask whether it's actually connected to the thing you're supposed to be delivering this week, or whether it just feels urgent because someone asked in real time. Urgency and importance get confused constantly, and most calendars end up optimized for the former at the expense of the latter.

The people who consistently get noticed for their impact aren't usually the busiest people in the room. They're the ones who can point to a small number of things that clearly moved, and explain in one sentence why those were the right things to spend the week on.
