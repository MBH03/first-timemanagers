Prepare for the conversation about readiness.

Eventually, you'll need to ask directly whether your company sees you as a future manager. Don't wait until you're frustrated, or until the role's already gone to someone else. Pick a calm moment and ask for an honest assessment.

Ask for an honest assessment.

Say it like this

"I've been working on leading projects, supporting teammates and improving our processes. I'd like to understand how close you think I am to being ready for a management role. What's the biggest gap I need to address?"

Hear the answer.

The answer might be encouraging, or it might not be. Your boss might tell you that you need:

  • More experience
  • Stronger communication skills
  • Better judgment under pressure
  • Or that there's no opening on the horizon at all

Either way, that's the answer you came for. Don't argue with it in the moment. Just listen and take it in.

If the feedback is specific, turn it into a plan. Ask what success would look like and when you should revisit the conversation.

If it's vague, ask for examples.

If your boss can't explain what you need to do to progress, that tells you something too. Maybe you need a different manager, a different team, or a different company.

Bring evidence, not hope.

Prepare for this conversation like a business discussion, not a request for reassurance. Bring evidence, describe what you've learned, and ask questions that get you specific answers instead of a vague "you're doing great."

Instead of: "I've worked hard, so I deserve a promotion."

Say: "Over the past six months, I led two projects, trained three new employees, and improved the handoff process. I'd like your assessment of the strengths I'm demonstrating and the gaps that still matter for management."

Readiness is not the same as opportunity.

Keep readiness and opportunity separate in your head. They're not the same question.

  • You might be ready for management but there's no open role.
  • Or there might be an opening but you still need development.

Ask your manager to address both, not just whichever one is easier to answer.

Readiness Conversation Template

"I would like your honest assessment of my readiness for management. Over the past few months, I have led [project], supported [person or team] and improved [process or result]. What strengths do you see, and what is the most important gap I should address?"

Follow up with:

"What specific behavior would demonstrate improvement?"
"How would you know I had made progress?"
"What opportunity could help me practice that skill?"
"What would make you comfortable recommending me?"
"Is there a realistic management opening in the next six to twelve months?"
"When should we discuss this again?"
"What should I do if the opportunity does not exist here?"

If the Feedback Is Vague

Try: "Could you give me a recent example of where I fell short of that expectation?" Then ask: "What would a stronger response have looked like?" Specific examples turn general criticism into something you can practice.

Tip: Bring Evidence

Before the conversation, prepare a short list of:

What you did. Projects you led, people you trained or mentored, processes you improved, problems you solved, results you helped create.

How you handled it. Examples of handling conflict or ambiguity, situations where you influenced people without formal authority.

What's still open. Feedback you received, skills you're still developing.

Keep the list concise. The goal is to support a productive conversation, not to overwhelm your manager with a long defense of your worth.

After the Conversation

Send a short summary. This creates accountability for both of you.

"Thank you for discussing my development today. My understanding is that I should focus on [skill] by taking on [responsibility]. We will review progress on [date]. I will measure improvement through [outcome or behavior]. Please let me know if I misunderstood anything."
