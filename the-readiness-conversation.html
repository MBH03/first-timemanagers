Prepare for the conversation about readiness.

Eventually, you will need to ask directly whether your company sees you as a future manager. Do not wait until you are frustrated or until a position has already been given to someone else. Choose a calm moment and ask for an honest assessment.

You could say: "I have been working on leading projects, supporting teammates and improving our processes. I would like to understand how close you think I am to being ready for a management role. What is the biggest gap I need to address?"

The answer may be encouraging. It may also be disappointing. Your boss may believe you need more experience, stronger communication skills or better judgment under pressure. They may tell you that there is no foreseeable opening. Listen carefully.

If the feedback is specific, turn it into a plan. Ask what success would look like and when you should revisit the conversation. If the feedback is vague, ask for examples. If your boss cannot explain what you need to do to progress, that is useful information too. It may mean you need a different manager, a different team or a different company.

Prepare for this conversation as if it were a business discussion, not a request for reassurance. Bring evidence, describe what you have learned and ask questions that produce specific answers.

Avoid saying: "I have worked hard, so I deserve a promotion." Instead, say: "Over the past six months, I led two projects, trained three new employees and improved the handoff process. I would like your assessment of the strengths I am demonstrating and the gaps that still matter for management." This approach makes the conversation more objective.

You should also distinguish between readiness and opportunity. You may be ready for management but lack an available role. You may have an opportunity but still need development. Ask your manager to address both questions separately.

Readiness Conversation Template

"I would like your honest assessment of my readiness for management. Over the past few months, I have led [project], supported [person or team] and improved [process or result]. What strengths do you see, and what is the most important gap I should address?"

Follow up with:

"What specific behavior would demonstrate improvement?"
"How would you know I had made progress?"
"What opportunity could help me practice that skill?"
"What would make you comfortable recommending me?"
"Is there a realistic management opening in the next six to twelve months?"
"When should we discuss this again?"
"What should I do if the opportunity does not exist here?"

If the Feedback Is Vague

Try: "Could you give me a recent example of where I fell short of that expectation?" Then ask: "What would a stronger response have looked like?" Specific examples turn general criticism into something you can practice.

Tip: Bring Evidence

Before the conversation, prepare a short list of: projects you led, people you trained or mentored, processes you improved, problems you solved, results you helped create, feedback you received, skills you are still developing, examples of handling conflict or ambiguity, situations where you influenced people without formal authority. Keep the list concise. The goal is to support a productive conversation, not to overwhelm your manager with a long defense of your worth.

After the Conversation

Send a short summary. This creates accountability for both of you.

"Thank you for discussing my development today. My understanding is that I should focus on [skill] by taking on [responsibility]. We will review progress on [date]. I will measure improvement through [outcome or behavior]. Please let me know if I misunderstood anything."
