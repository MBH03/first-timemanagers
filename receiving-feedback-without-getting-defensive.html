The first sixty seconds after hard feedback.

You're sitting there, and someone just said the thing you didn't want to hear. Your face is doing something you can't control. Your brain is already building a defense.

That next 60 seconds decides if the feedback actually changes anything.

1. Don't respond in the same conversation.

Your first reaction to criticism is rarely your best one.

My go to line:

Say this

“That's worth sitting with. Let me think about it and come back to you tomorrow.”

That's a complete sentence. You don't have to fill the silence to prove you're not rattled. What comes out in the first 30 seconds is almost always defensiveness plus performance. Neither is what you want on record.

2. Separate the delivery from the content.

Feedback can be true and delivered badly. Those are two different problems.

It's tempting to throw out the whole message because you didn't like the tone. Because the tone is what actually hurt.

But someone being blunt, or catching you off guard, or picking a bad moment doesn't make them wrong. Deal with the delivery separately, if it's even worth raising. Deal with the content on its own.

3. Ask one clarifying question, not five defensive ones.

A clarifying question gets you information. A defensive one gets you a debate.

“Can you give me a specific example?” is clarifying. “Well, what about the time when...” is a rebuttal with a question mark.

In the moment they feel the same. They're not.

Use this:

Say this

“Can you walk me through a specific example of when that showed up? I want to make sure I understand exactly what you're seeing.”

4. Come back with what you're actually going to do differently.

Not an apology. A specific change.

There's a huge difference between “You're right, I'll be better” and “I'm going to send the agenda a day ahead of every meeting starting this week.”

The second one is the only one people actually notice. An apology costs nothing and changes nothing. Most people who give hard feedback have heard plenty of apologies that went nowhere.

A specific, observable change is what closes the loop.

After the Feedback: A Quick Worksheet

Fill this out in the gap you bought yourself, before you go back with a response.

What was actually said, as close to their words as I can get it (not my interpretation)
Is there a delivery issue worth raising separately, or is that not actually the point
One clarifying question I still need answered
The specific, observable thing I'm going to do differently, starting when

This week: write down the last piece of hard feedback you got, as close to their words as you can remember. Then ask yourself which part was true.
