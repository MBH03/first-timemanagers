Knowing when it's actually time to leave.

A genuine dead end and a rough stretch look alike from the inside. Four tests tell them apart.

01
Separate a bad month from a bad fit
One rough stretch is not a verdict on the whole job. Look at a rolling pattern over a quarter, not the week you happened to ask yourself the question.

Test it: has this been true for three months running, or just since the last hard project landed on your desk? Those call for different answers.
02
Name what you're actually optimizing for
Growth, money, stability, and title all trade off differently. Get specific about which one is actually missing before you go looking for the next thing.

Finish this sentence honestly: "I would stay if only ___." If the blank is fixable where you are, that is worth knowing before you start applying anywhere else.
03
Have the internal conversation before the external one
Tell your manager what is not working before you tell a recruiter. Most fixable problems never actually get raised out loud, and that is usually why they stay unfixed.

"I want to be upfront that I've been thinking about whether this role is still the right fit. Before I decide anything, I wanted to talk to you about [the specific thing] first."
04
Don't let a counteroffer be the reason you stay
A counteroffer fixes the money, not whatever made you start looking. Ask yourself honestly if you would still want to leave even if the raise was not on the table.

If the honest answer is yes, take the exit you were already planning. A bigger number rarely fixes a role that was never the problem's real cause.

If you've run all four and the answer still points to the door, the next move is leaving well, not just leaving.

If the Answer Is Leave, and It's a Different Field

Leaving for a new industry or function raises two more questions before you touch your resume.

01
Translate your track record, don't just list it
"Managed a team of six" means nothing without the outcome attached, and it means even less translated into a field that doesn't use your old vocabulary. Rewrite your experience in the language of the field you're moving into, not the one you're leaving.

Instead of "Ran the onboarding process for enterprise clients," try "Built the repeatable process that took new customers from signed contract to first value in under three weeks." The second version works in almost any industry.
02
Get one real conversation with someone who made the jump
Job postings will not tell you what actually matters in the new field. A thirty minute conversation with someone who has already crossed saves you months of guessing.

"I'm exploring a move into [field] from [field]. Would you have twenty minutes to tell me what surprised you most about the switch?" Most people say yes to that question.

Have the one conversation before you touch your resume. It will tell you which parts of your story are worth translating.
