Workplace Skills · Judgment

Asking for a promotion isn't the same conversation as asking for more responsibility. Here's the actual ask.

Plenty of people build the track record and then never have the direct conversation, hoping it gets noticed and offered on its own. Sometimes it does. Often it doesn't, simply because nobody said the sentence out loud. There are two halves to this: building the case, then making the ask.

Build The Case

Time in the role is a clock, not evidence. "I've been doing this for two years" feels like a case. It isn't one. What actually counts is whether you're already doing pieces of the next role, without the title, often without being asked.

A useful exercise

List three things the next level does that you don't. Be honest about the gap, not "I could do that if given the chance," but "am I already doing a version of that now." If that list comes back mostly empty, the conversation to have first is about readiness, not the ask.

Promotions usually confirm work that's already happening. They rarely create it. So document the impact as it happens, not at review time, memory is a bad archive, and "I did a lot of good work this year" is a feeling, not a case. Log one line a week: what you did, what changed because of it, and the number if there is one. Six months later, "I improved onboarding" is forgettable. "I cut onboarding time from three weeks to eight days, which meant two new reps hit quota a full month earlier" is not.

Alongside the log, start operating at the next level before you ask for the title. Look at what someone one level up actually does day to day, not what the job description says, and do pieces of it now. By the time you ask, the promotion should feel like paperwork catching up to reality, not a leap of faith.

Find a sponsor, too, not just a mentor. A mentor gives you advice; a sponsor advocates for you in rooms you're not in, and they're rarely the same person. Don't ask "will you be my sponsor," that's rarely how it's framed. Keep them genuinely informed on your wins as they happen, so when a slot opens and you're not there to make the case yourself, they already have it in hand.

Promotion Case Builder

Fill this out now, not the week before the conversation.

Wins logged this cycle, with numbers attached
What the next level up actually does, that I'm already doing pieces of
My target title and timeframe
Who my sponsor is, or who it could be if I don't have one yet
The exact ask I'd make if I had the conversation tomorrow
Make The Ask Out Loud

None of it counts if you never have the direct conversation. Request dedicated time instead of dropping it into an unrelated meeting, that signals you take it seriously, and gives your manager a chance to prepare instead of reacting cold. Then lead with your case, not with a question about whether you're ready, the second framing puts your manager in the position of evaluating you cold, while the first gives them something concrete to respond to.

Example: the ask

"I'd like to set aside time in our next 1:1, or a separate slot if that's easier, to talk about my path to [title]. Here's the case I'd make: [two or three specific pieces of impact, with the numbers attached]. I'd like to get there by [timeframe], what would need to be true for that to happen, and what am I missing?" That last question matters as much as the ask itself. It turns the conversation into a shared plan, and surfaces the gap, if there is one, while there's still time to close it.

"Do you think I'm ready?" invites a maybe. "Here's my case, what do you think?" invites an actual answer.

Be ready for one of three responses, and know your next move for each. A yes, with a real process and timeline attached, ask for specifics, in writing if possible. A "not yet," with a clear, specific gap named, treat that gap as the actual roadmap, and revisit it in a set number of months, not indefinitely. A vague non-answer with no real reasoning, that's worth pressing on directly: "can you help me understand specifically what's missing, so I know what to work on?"

Decide beforehand what you'll do if the honest answer, after a fair process, is "not here, not soon." That's not a failure on your part, sometimes the timing, budget, or org structure genuinely isn't there, independent of your performance. Knowing in advance whether you're willing to wait, or whether that answer sends you looking elsewhere, keeps you from being blindsided by your own reaction in the moment.
