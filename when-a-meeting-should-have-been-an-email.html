Home / Workplace Skills / When a Meeting Should Have Been an Email
Workplace Skills · Meetings

The meeting that should have been an email isn't a joke. It's a real, avoidable cost.

A thirty-minute meeting with six people isn't thirty minutes — it's three hours of collective time, spent to deliver something that often could have been read in ninety seconds.

The habit worth building, as the person calling a meeting, is a quick test before it goes on anyone's calendar: is this meeting for making a decision together, working through something that genuinely needs real-time back-and-forth, or resolving disagreement that hasn't moved over email? If the honest answer is "no, I just need to tell people something," it's not a meeting. It's a message.

Example — a meeting that should have been an email

A recurring "status update" meeting where each person reports what they did that week, with no discussion, no decisions, and no disagreement to work through — just information moving one direction, from several people to one shared audience.

Example — a meeting that's actually needed

A planning session where three people with different constraints need to negotiate a shared timeline in real time, because the tradeoffs depend on questions that only surface through back-and-forth discussion.

The difference isn't the topic — it's whether the value comes from information moving, or from people actually working something out together. Status updates, FYIs, and one-directional announcements almost always belong in writing. Anything that requires genuine negotiation, working through ambiguity together, or reading a room's reaction in real time usually needs the meeting.

If nobody in the room needs to say anything back, it wasn't a meeting. It was an announcement wearing a calendar invite.

If you're on the receiving end of a meeting that seems like it should have been an email, raising that directly — respectfully, and ideally before it becomes a recurring habit — is worth doing. "Would it work to send this as an update instead, so we can save the time for things that need discussion?" is a reasonable question that most people appreciate being asked, even if they hadn't thought to ask it themselves.

One nuance worth holding onto: some meetings that look purely informational actually serve a real, if unstated, purpose — building team cohesion, giving quieter voices a regular chance to be heard, or simply keeping a distributed team feeling connected. Those aren't wasted meetings even without a decision on the agenda. The test isn't "did a decision get made" — it's "did this need to happen live, or could it have happened just as well in writing."
