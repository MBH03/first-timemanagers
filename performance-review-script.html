The First-Time Manager's Guide to Scripting a Performance Review

Your first review season is nerve-wracking for a reason. You've never had to sit across from someone and tell them, clearly, how they're doing, and have it actually land instead of confuse them, deflate them, or go in one ear and out the other.

Here's the good news: you don't need to be a natural. You need a structure. Use this as a starting point, not something to read verbatim. Say it in your own words, but keep the order and the directness.

Why Structure Matters More Than Wording

Most first-time managers over-prepare the words and under-prepare the shape. They write a paragraph, memorize it, and then panic the second the conversation goes off script, because it always does.

The fix isn't a better script. It's a framework loose enough to hold a real conversation but tight enough that you never lose the thread. Four moves, in this order, every time.

1. Set Up the Structure

"Before we get into specifics, here's how this review is structured and why."

Tell them what's coming before you say any of it. People spend the first two minutes of a review bracing for bad news instead of listening, because they don't know what shape the conversation is going to take. Naming the structure up front removes that anxiety, so they can actually hear the content instead of just surviving it.

2. Strengths, Grounded in Specifics

"Here's what's working, and here's the specific example that shows it."

A generic compliment lands as filler. "You're doing great" tells them nothing and they'll forget it by lunch. "The way you handled the client escalation in March, staying calm while pulling in the right people, that's exactly the judgment call I want to see more of" tells them something real, something they can point to and repeat. Come in with the example already picked. Don't improvise it in the room.

3. Growth Area, Named Directly

"Here's the one area I want you focused on this cycle, and here's why it matters."

Pick one. A list of five growth areas is not a plan, it's a burden, and nobody walks out of a review able to actually act on all five. This is the part first-time managers soften the most, and it's the part that needs the least softening. Directness here is a kindness. Vagueness is what actually feels bad in hindsight.

4. Close With the Throughline

"Here's how everything we just talked about connects to where you're headed."

Don't end on the growth area, that leaves them sitting with the criticism as the last thing they heard. Connect it back: this is what's working, this is the one thing to focus on, and here's how that lines up with where you want to go next. Send them out with a direction, not just a scorecard.

Two Things to Have Ready Before You Walk In

A plan for pushback. If they disagree or get defensive, don't abandon the structure, slow down inside it. "Tell me more about how you're seeing it" buys you a real answer without you having to cave or dig in.

Silence you're willing to sit in. After you name the growth area, stop talking. First-time managers tend to fill the quiet with qualifiers and walk back half of what they just said. Let the sentence land. Let them respond.

The Whole Thing, in Order
1. Structure
Tell them what's coming.
2. Strengths
Specific, not generic.
3. Growth area
One, named directly.
4. Throughline
Connect it to where they're headed.

Say it in your own words. Keep the order. The order is what makes it a framework instead of a script, it holds up even when the conversation doesn't go the way you rehearsed it.
