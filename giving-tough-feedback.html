You need to give feedback that's going to sting.

A colleague gave me some feedback recently. It wasn't easy to hear. The higher you sit in an organization, the less likely you are to hear the truth, and it works the same way in reverse: the more senior you get as a manager, the more your reports quietly brace before telling you something you don't want to hear.

Have you ever done a cold plunge? Feedback that actually stings works a lot like one, for the person giving it as much as the person receiving it. Your instinct screams to soften it, delay it, or wrap it in enough padding that the point gets lost. But the discomfort is the signal that it matters. Feedback that doesn't sting a little usually wasn't specific enough to be useful.

01
Say it in the same week you notice it
Feedback that waits a month has lost its context and gained resentment. The longer you sit on it, the more it curdles into something bigger than the original issue.
02
Be specific and timely, not vague
"You need to be better at communication" isn't useful to anyone. "In today's meeting, you interrupted twice, try pausing before responding next time" is something they can actually act on.
03
Focus on the action, not the character
People shut down the moment they feel attacked. "You're bad at presenting" invites defensiveness. "This would land stronger if you slowed down and made more eye contact" invites a fix.
04
Balance the critique with what's actually working
Effective feedback builds, it doesn't just break. Naming a real strength alongside the area to improve is what makes someone willing to act on the hard part instead of just absorbing the blow.

The sting isn't a sign you did it wrong. It's usually a sign there was truth in it, for them and, when you're the one receiving feedback yourself, for you too. Say it clearly, mean it kindly, and don't apologize for the feedback itself, just for any unkindness in how you delivered it.
