Workplace Skills · Meetings

A good question in a meeting does more work than a good comment.

A sharp comment shows you understood the material. A sharp question can actually change the outcome of the meeting, and it's remembered longer, because it's the thing the room ends up discussing.

Most meeting questions fall into one of two weak categories: the clarifying question that could have been answered by reading the doc beforehand, or the "just to play devil's advocate" question that isn't really trying to get anywhere, just to signal engagement. Both are forgettable. The questions people remember are the ones that surface a real gap, an assumption nobody's tested, a risk nobody's named, a tradeoff nobody's made explicit.

The best question in the room isn't the hardest one. It's the one nobody else was going to ask, and everyone needed answered.

Example: weak

"Just to clarify, when you say Q3, do you mean calendar Q3 or fiscal Q3?"

Example: strong

"This plan assumes the vendor integration is done by Q3, what happens to the rest of the timeline if that slips, and do we have a fallback if it does?"

The strong version does three things the weak one doesn't: it names an assumption everyone in the room was quietly relying on, it asks about a consequence that actually matters, and it invites the room to solve something rather than just clarify a detail. Asking it doesn't require special expertise, it requires actually listening for what the plan is assuming rather than just what it's stating.

A reliable way to generate this kind of question: as you listen, mentally track what's being assumed rather than said outright. Every plan rests on assumptions the presenter didn't have time to state explicitly, dependencies, timelines, who's available, what "success" specifically means. Picking one of those and asking about it directly is almost always more valuable than a question about the parts that were already spelled out.

Timing and tone matter as much as content. A genuinely good question asked in a way that sounds like an attack, "did anyone actually think about X?", gets a defensive answer instead of a real one. The same question asked with real curiosity, "I might be missing something, but how does this account for X?", gets an honest answer, and makes it more likely you'll be the person asked to weigh in next time.
