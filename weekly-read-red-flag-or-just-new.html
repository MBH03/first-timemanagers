Onboarding

Is It a Red Flag, or Are They Just New?

M
From Mike Henrie
The Question

Early on, how do you know if something you're seeing in a new hire is a real red flag or just new job ramp?

The Answer

Red flags are real. First impressions do matter. But I'm careful how much I read into them early.

If a new hire is pushing back, honestly, maybe they just hate feeling lost or confused. It's kind of their way to take the pressure off themselves.

If they don't seem engaged, they might just not know what to do. They could be nervous. You're the new member of a team that's already existed. People know stuff, you're new. Maybe they seem disinterested because they don't know what to do next.

This is critically untalked about for a lot of companies: your first 90 day onboarding plan can make or break someone's success. Whether your company does this or not, you take it on yourself to build the perfect 90 day plan.

Here's the template I use, free, no signup: first-timemanagers.com

How I set up the first 90 days

Every new hire gets an onboarding buddy. Assigned for 90 days. Required to meet at least once a week. I tell the new hire: this is the person you go to with questions. If you don't understand something, if you get stuck, you don't have to wait till your meeting. This is the person you're going to Slack with throughout the day. I make sure that buddy has time to help and can be thoughtful about it.

I've learned people would much rather ask a coworker a question, especially if they think it's a dumb question, than ask their boss. This has been one of the biggest changes I've made.

I map out their first week in detail. Like every two hour block what they should be working on.

In that, I set up five intro calls. I put it on both people's calendar. I let them know I'm not showing up. They can use my meeting link. Usually starts day two. The new person just shows up to listen.

I give the other person a list of things to cover:

  • Walk them through our knowledge base, HR portal, org chart and team structure. Start with get to know you: where did you both come from?
  • Walk through X process all the way through, steps 1 through 10, with details.
  • Do a kickoff demo for the new hire. Let them take notes. Let them record it if they want to.

These first five are locked in. No pressure on the new hire, they're showing up and learning from different people.

The next five intro calls, usually week two and three, I tell them who to meet with, but I put it in their hands. You need to schedule these, and here are the topics I want you to cover.

Milestones throughout the 90 days. For example:

  • Do all 10 of your intro one on one calls
  • Read through the knowledge base
  • Do a mock kickoff call to the team
  • Set up time with me to explain X, Y, Z as you would explain it to a customer
  • Research competitors and understand the marketplace
  • Learn the language we use. If it's medical, make sure you understand the new words

Progressively let them go. Week one and two is pretty scheduled. Then it gets less and less. At week six, I'm going to give you your first client or first project. Could be week four, could be week two, depending on how complex your process is. Lay out how you're going to ramp them.

I leave free time in there. Time to go play around, search, play in a demo environment. But I am absolutely watching this.

This helps you truly understand if the flags you're seeing are concerning or just someone getting used to it.

When it is a red flag

If I see missing milestones, not setting up calls, not doing what they're supposed to do, instant red flag. Address it early. Very direct, with detail. It should be in a Word doc you both can open at any time. They can write notes in it, leave links, check it off.

So you can pull it up and say:

Say It Like This

"Hey, this was due last week. I noticed you didn't do it. Help me understand why. Help me understand why you didn't bring this to me. Just so you know, if I give you a due date, I want it done. We take project timelines very seriously here."

Don't be mean about it, but address problems early.

If you start to see a pattern, you'll know pretty quick if you need to terminate. Depending on your company policy, put them on a PIP, which I personally wouldn't do if someone's only been there six months. I'd try to let them go, maybe with a couple weeks of severance if possible.

What a good sign looks like

Structure it where you know they can be successful. Then you can look for signs:

  • Are they starting to get more comfortable in meetings?
  • Are they pushing back less?
  • Are they getting more involved in team chit chat?

Maybe you learned they're on the quieter side, which is fine, but when they speak, it's generally interesting.

If the flag is still there after clear expectations and a real example, then you've got a real answer.
