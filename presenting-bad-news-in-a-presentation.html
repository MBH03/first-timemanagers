Home / Workplace Skills / How to Present Bad News
Workplace Skills · Presentations

A slide deck with bad news buried on slide fourteen isn't being tactful. It's making the room wait for the part that actually matters.

The instinct to build up to bad news slowly — context first, analysis second, the actual headline last — is understandable, but in a presentation it usually backfires. A room that senses something's coming spends the first thirteen slides bracing instead of listening.

The structure that works puts the headline on the first substantive slide, stated plainly, followed immediately by what it means and what you're proposing to do about it. This isn't about being cold — it's about respecting that the room's attention is a limited resource, and spending most of it on setup means less is left for the part that actually requires their engagement: the plan.

Example — buried

A twelve-slide deck that opens with market context, then quarterly performance, then a deep dive into individual metrics, before finally landing on slide ten: "as you can see, we're behind on the Q3 target."

Example — led with

Slide one: "We're behind on the Q3 target — currently at 72% of goal, with six weeks left." Slide two: "Here's why, and here's the plan to close the gap." The remaining slides provide the supporting detail, available but not required to understand the headline.

The second structure gives the room the actual news immediately, then spends the rest of the presentation on the part that needs their attention most: what happens next. It also signals confidence — a presenter who leads with the hard part reads as someone in control of the situation, not someone still working up the nerve to say it.

A deck that saves the bad news for the end isn't softening the blow. It's just making the room sit through thirteen slides of suspense to get there.

Pair the headline with a plan on the very next slide, not several slides later. Bad news with no visible plan attached leaves a room anxious and unmoored; bad news immediately followed by "here's what we're doing about it" gives them somewhere to direct that energy. Even an incomplete plan — "here are the three options we're evaluating" — is better than bad news floating alone with nothing attached to it.

Keep the tone matter-of-fact rather than apologetic throughout. Repeated apologies within a presentation shift the room's focus from the problem and the plan to managing your discomfort about presenting it, which is a worse use of everyone's time than the news itself deserved.
