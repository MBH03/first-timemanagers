Career Advice

Making the case for a promotion, before you have to ask.

Most people wait. They keep their head down, do good work, and assume someone above them is quietly keeping score. Then review season comes, nothing changes, and they're left wondering what they were supposed to have done differently.

Here's the uncomfortable truth: nobody is keeping that score for you. You are. Four moves for making the case before you have to ask, not a general essay on being patient.

01
Document impact as it happens, not at review time
Keep a running list of wins with numbers attached. Memory is a bad archive, you will forget the specifics that made the case strong, and "I did a lot of good work this year" is not a case, it's a feeling. One line, logged the week it happens: what you did, what changed because of it, and the number if there is one. Six months later, "I improved onboarding" is forgettable. "I cut onboarding time from three weeks to eight days, which meant two new reps hit quota a full month earlier" is not.
02
Do the level above before you ask for the title
Promotions usually confirm work that's already happening. They rarely create it. Start operating at the next level before the conversation, not after, asking for a title you haven't demonstrated yet puts your manager in the position of betting on you instead of backing you. Look at what someone one level up actually does day to day, not what the job description says, and start doing pieces of it now. By the time you ask, the promotion should feel like paperwork catching up to reality, not a leap of faith.
03
Make the ask explicit
"I want to be promoted, and here's my case" is a completely different conversation than hoping someone eventually notices. Say it directly, with a timeline attached. Vague hints, working extra hours and waiting to be noticed, dropping "I've been really growing lately" into a one-on-one, rarely turn into an actual promotion, because nobody's on the hook to act on a hint.
04
Find a sponsor, not just a mentor
A mentor gives you advice. A sponsor advocates for you in rooms you're not in. You need both, and they're rarely the same person. A mentor tells you how to handle a hard conversation. A sponsor is the one who says your name when a promotion slot opens and you're not there to make the case yourself. Don't ask "will you be my sponsor," that's rarely how it's framed. Instead, keep them genuinely informed on your wins as they happen, so when the moment comes, they already have the case in hand.
The Ask, Scripted

"I want to talk about my path to [title]. Here's the case I'd make: [two or three specific pieces of impact]. I'd like to get there by [timeframe], what would need to be true for that to happen, and what am I missing?" That last question matters as much as the ask itself. It turns the conversation from a request into a shared plan, and it surfaces the gap, if there is one, while there's still time to close it.
