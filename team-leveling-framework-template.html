The Team Leveling Framework Template

What this is
A spreadsheet that defines what good work looks like at each level on your team, across the skills that actually matter, so a promotion isn't based on a feeling.
When to use it
Build it once, before your next round of promotion or leveling conversations, not in the middle of one.
Common mistake
Writing competencies as personality traits instead of visible behavior. "Is proactive" doesn't tell anyone what to actually do differently; "flags a blocker the same day" does.
How to use this
  1. Download the Excel file.
  2. Set the levels your team really uses on the Levels tab, then rewrite the Framework tab in your own words, three short bullets per box.
  3. Use the optional Measures tab to back the framework with real numbers, and the "Ready for the next level when" row to flag promotion timing.

A one time job. Plan on about 30 minutes. Set the levels your team really uses, then write what good looks like at each one, across the skills that actually matter, so the next promotion conversation starts from a shared answer instead of a guess.

Most teams never write this down. Readiness lives in one manager's head, so two people doing the same work can get a different answer depending on who they ask. A leveling framework turns that into something everyone can see and point to.

What It's Good For
Promotion conversations
Turns a vague "are they ready?" into a checklist you can point to, with both sides looking at the same bar.
Consistent expectations across managers
If two managers on the same team level people differently, this is the document that gets them to agree.
Hiring at the right level
Interview panels can rate a candidate against the same bullets a current employee is rated against.
Growth conversations
Gives a specific next skill to build instead of a general "keep doing great work."
How to Build One
  1. Name your real levels. Four are filled in as an example, Associate through Manager. Rename them, reorder them, or leave extras blank to match your own team.
  2. Read each example skill, then rewrite it in your own words. Keep it to three short bullets per box. If you can't picture someone actually doing it, cut it.
  3. Make sure each level clearly builds on the one before it. If two boxes in a row read the same, one of them is wrong.
  4. Add real measures where you have them. The Measures tab is optional, use it to back the framework with numbers, and write "n/a" where a measure doesn't apply to a level.
  5. Share the draft with your team before you call it final. Their edits will make it more believable than anything you write alone.

Five skills is plenty. More than seven and nobody remembers them.

Quick Tips
Do
  • Write what a person does that others can see, not what they are. "Flags a blocker the same day" beats "is proactive."
  • Make sure each level clearly builds on the one before it.
  • Share the draft with your team before you call it final.
Don't
  • Don't leave two levels in a row reading the same, if they do, one of them is wrong.
  • Don't write competencies in a vacuum, the example text is written for a support or operations team, change it to fit your own function.
  • Don't skip the Measures tab entirely if you already have real numbers, it makes the framework harder to argue with.
