How to Make Your Numbers Say Something

A chart with no conclusion attached asks the room to guess. Most won't guess right. They'll either misread the headline or check out until the next slide.

Numbers don't speak for themselves. Not to a room seeing them for the first time. Presenting data "neutrally" feels safer, but it just hands the hardest part of the job back to the room. Someone has to say what the number means. That's your job, not theirs.

Example: data without a conclusion

A chart showing churn ticking up over three quarters, labeled "Churn Rate, Q1–Q3" and nothing else.

Example: data with a conclusion

The same chart, titled "Churn is up for the third straight quarter. This is a trend, not noise." with one line underneath: "Driven mainly by customers in their first 90 days."

The second version names what the numbers mean instead of leaving the room to guess. If someone disagrees with your read, they can say so. That's a far better conversation than a room silently guessing whether your chart is good news or bad.

A number without a conclusion is a Rorschach test. Everyone in the room sees something different, and most of it isn't what you meant.

One chart, one point. Five points means five slides, each with its own headline and its own moment of attention, not one dashboard trying to carry all five at once.

Say It in Four Sentences First

Before you build the deck, write the executive summary. Situation, recommendation, reasoning, ask, in four sentences. If someone reads only that paragraph, they should still know what happened, what you want, why, and what you need from them.

Example: a weak opener to avoid

"This document covers our Q3 performance, an analysis of the market, and a recommended path forward." That's a table of contents, not a summary. It tells the room nothing it couldn't have guessed on its own.

01
Situation
"Q3 revenue came in 8% below plan, driven mainly by a slower enterprise sales cycle."
02
Recommendation
"We recommend shifting two headcount from mid-market to enterprise support in Q4."
03
Reasoning
"This addresses the slowdown directly instead of waiting for the market to correct on its own."
04
Ask
"We need a decision on the headcount shift by October 1st to hit the Q4 timeline."

Be honest about uncertainty instead of hiding behind false precision. "Roughly 15%, depending on how we define active users" is more trustworthy than a suspiciously exact "14.7%" that implies confidence the data doesn't support. Executives who work with numbers regularly can tell the difference, and overstating precision costs you credibility the moment it's noticed.

Next chart you build, write the sentence underneath it before you touch the slide.
