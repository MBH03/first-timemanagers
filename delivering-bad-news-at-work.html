How to Give Bad News to an Employee

People can handle bad news. What they can't handle is a winding explanation that leaves them guessing what you're trying to say.

Most new managers do this. You know the news will land badly, so you pad it with some context, a compliment, and a "just wanted to touch base." Three minutes in, the person still doesn't know if they're in trouble. Then you finally say it, and it feels like an ambush.

The Bad Version
Say It Like This

"Hey, got a minute? I wanted to chat about the Henderson project. You've been working really hard and everyone appreciates it. A few things came up, and I'm not saying anyone did anything wrong, but the client had some thoughts about the timeline, and maybe we could look at how to approach things going forward."

Two minutes later they're asking, "Wait, is this bad?"

The Direct Version
Say It Like This

"The Henderson deliverable went out two days late and the client noticed. I wanted you to hear it from me. From here on I need a heads up the moment a deadline is at risk, not after it passes. What got in the way?"

The second one is shorter, and the person knows exactly where they stand. They also know you're still on their side, because you asked a question instead of handing down a verdict.

The Playbook

1. Use the 24 hour rule. If you're annoyed, give yourself a minute to cool off. Bad news delivered while you're angry comes out as blame. But don't wait longer than 24 hours. The longer you sit on it, the more it becomes a surprise for them and a secret for you.

2. Pick the right setting. Do it in person or on a call, in private.

3. Write it down first. Before you get on the call or walk into the room, write down the one sentence that says the news, and what you plan to say after it. Then open with that sentence. Skip the warm up.

4. Bring receipts. Receipts are the dates and specifics behind whatever you're about to talk about. "The report went out Thursday with the wrong revenue figures" works. "You've been sloppy lately" doesn't. It keeps you from sounding like you're making things up.

5. Stop talking and let them react. Once the news is delivered, stop. Let them react, then listen. Don't argue with them and don't pile on more examples.

6. Finish with what happens next. End the conversation with a plan. What you need from them, by when, and what you'll do to help.

7. Document it and follow up. Send a short email after. What you discussed, what you agreed to. Three lines is plenty.

This week: next time you see an employee do something wrong, address it right away with these steps.
