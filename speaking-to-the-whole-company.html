Workplace Skills · Presentations

The first time you have to speak in front of the whole company.

This is a different kind of nervous than presenting to your own team or even a room of executives. Most of the audience doesn't know you yet, and for a good chunk of them, this five minutes is the entire dataset they'll use to decide what kind of manager you are.

It's worth naming that plainly, because pretending it's "just a presentation" makes people over-prepare the content and under-prepare the delivery. The content is usually the easy part, an update, a milestone, an introduction to your team's work. What actually goes wrong is nerves showing up as speed, hedging, or an apology for being new, none of which have anything to do with whether the update itself was good.

What's Actually Different Here

In a team meeting, someone will ask a clarifying question if you lose them, and a slip gets forgotten by lunch. In an all-hands, nobody interrupts, so unclear stays unclear, and because most of the room doesn't have other data points on you yet, whatever you show them carries more weight than it would with people who already know your work.

Don't apologize for being new
"I'm still getting up to speed, but..." trains the room to discount everything that follows it. They already know you're new. Say what you came to say.
Say less than you think you should
A packed five minutes reads as nervous. A focused two minutes reads as someone who knew exactly what mattered. Cut until it hurts a little.
Have one message, not five
If someone asked a coworker afterward "what did they say," there should be one clean sentence available as the answer, not a summary of everything you covered.
Rehearse the first 30 seconds out loud, once
Not the whole thing, just the opening. Nerves front-load into the start, get the first few sentences into your body and the rest tends to follow on its own.
A Simple Opening That Works

"Hi everyone, I'm [Name], I lead [team/area]. Today I want to share [one thing], why it matters, and what's next." Four sentences. Say it, then move into the content. No preamble, no apology, no "sorry, is this working" for longer than it takes to fix it.

One more thing worth planning for: the Q&A, if there is one, is a bigger part of how you're read than the prepared portion. A confident "I don't know, let me find out and follow up" lands better than a stretched answer to a question you weren't ready for. Nobody in that room expects you to have every number memorized in week one.
