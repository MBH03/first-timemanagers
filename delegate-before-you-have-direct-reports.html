Learn to delegate before you have direct reports.

Delegation is one of the hardest management skills to develop because high performers often believe they can do the work faster themselves. They may be right. Doing everything yourself may be faster in the short term. But it prevents other people from learning, creates a bottleneck, and makes the team dependent on you.

You can practice delegation even when nobody reports to you.

If you are coordinating a project, do not keep every task for yourself. Match responsibilities to people's strengths, interests and development goals. Someone who is learning data analysis may benefit from owning the reporting task. Someone who communicates well may be suited to coordinating stakeholders.

Explain the outcome you need, provide the necessary context and give people room to complete the work. Do not disappear after assigning the task. Check in at reasonable points, answer questions and remove obstacles. But do not take the work back the moment someone approaches it differently than you would.

Good delegation is not simply handing off tasks. It is creating the conditions for someone else to succeed. That means accepting that the final result may not look exactly like the result you would have produced. If you cannot tolerate that difference, you are not delegating. You are trying to control.

Delegation also requires judgment. You should not delegate work without considering the person's experience, the consequences of failure and the support available. A beginner may need a smaller assignment, clearer examples and more frequent check-ins. An experienced colleague may need only the outcome, constraints and deadline.

The goal is not to abandon responsibility. The person completing the task owns the work, but you still own the quality of the delegation process. Before assigning something, ask:

  • Is the outcome clear?
  • Does the person have the authority and resources needed?
  • Have I explained why the work matters?
  • Is the deadline realistic?
  • What decisions can they make independently?
  • When should we check in?
  • What risks should they raise immediately?
Delegation Template

Use this structure when assigning a task.

The outcome: "Here is what we need to accomplish."
The context: "Here is why this matters and who will use the result."
The boundaries: "These are the requirements, limitations and deadlines."
The ownership: "You are responsible for completing this."
The authority: "You can make these decisions independently. Please bring these decisions back to me."
The support: "I am available to answer questions and remove obstacles."
The check-in: "Let's review progress on [date]."
The completion standard: "Here is what a successful result should include."

Example Delegation Script

"I would like you to own the first draft of the client onboarding guide. The goal is to make the process easier for new clients and reduce repeated questions. Please include the five steps we discussed, use the existing brand format and have a draft ready by Friday. You can decide how to organize the sections, but bring any policy questions to me. Let's check in Wednesday afternoon so I can help remove any obstacles."

Delegation Mistakes to Avoid
  • Assigning work without explaining why it matters
  • Giving unclear deadlines
  • Checking in so often that the person cannot work independently
  • Taking the task back too quickly
  • Criticizing a different but effective approach
  • Delegating only unpleasant tasks
  • Failing to recognize the person's contribution
  • Assigning responsibility without giving authority
  • Assuming silence means everything is on track
  • Giving feedback only at the end
  • Delegating work that you have not defined clearly yourself
How to Check In Without Micromanaging

Instead of asking, "Are you done yet?" ask: "What is going well?" · "What is unclear?" · "What risks do you see?" · "What decision do you need from me?" · "Are you still on track for the agreed deadline?" · "Is there anything blocking progress?" These questions provide support without taking ownership away from the other person.

Fun fact: delegation is not just a productivity skill. It is also a development tool. When you delegate thoughtfully, you give someone a chance to practice a skill they may need for their own career growth.
