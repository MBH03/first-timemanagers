Make It Simple Without Making It Sound Easy

How do you take something messy and complicated and make it feel simple, without making it sound like it was easy? I've been a manager for almost 15 years, and this is the skill I still have to work at. Every week.

I learned this the hard way as a software implementation manager. We were really good at delivery, we hit our milestones. But what nobody saw was what lived underneath each one: fifteen to twenty subtasks per milestone, and some of those had their own little subtasks hiding underneath. It was a beast to manage.

In the beginning I was proud of that beast. I would walk in with the full plan, the trackers, the dependencies, all of it. In my head I thought, if they see how much we're managing, they'll trust us. But I could feel the room drift. Eyes glaze over. People nodding but not really with me.

The shift happened when we started winning bigger clients. Suddenly I'm presenting to folks at Amazon, Fidelity, big logos where you don't get a second chance. I'd walk into these massive conference rooms and it was everyone: the technical lead who wants to poke holes, the benefits expert who lives in the details, the project manager with their own timeline, the data person, and the executive sponsor in the back who just wants to know, are we going to be okay?

I was nervous. Honestly, I over prepared. I had every subtask memorized in case someone tried to trip me up. What I learned was, they didn't want my anxiety and my spreadsheet. They wanted calm. They wanted someone to carry the complexity for them.

So we changed how we showed up. High level milestones up front. Clear story. Then we'd pause and say, happy to go deep anywhere you want. And they did. The experts would pick a spot and we'd go there together. The execs could finally breathe and see the whole journey.

Here's where we started, here's where we are, here's what's next.

I still had all 20 subtasks in my back pocket. I just stopped leading with them because I needed to prove something. That feeling when the room relaxes because you made it clear, that's what I'm still chasing.

What the Great Ones Do

Steve Jobs put words to it:

"Simple can be harder than complex. You have to work hard to get your thinking clean to make it simple. But it's worth it in the end because once you get there, you can move mountains."

That's leadership. Not pretending it's simple. Doing the hard thinking so your team and your customer don't have to wade through the mess. Three stories I think about a lot:

Jobs and the iPod
The team was drowning in interface options. Jobs kept asking, why do we need this screen at all? Do we really need this button? He wasn't coding it himself. He was stripping it back until the right answer was obvious. That's a different kind of courage.
Bezos and the one question
"When things get complicated, we simplify by asking: what's best for the customer?" I love that because when you're in the weeds, you need an anchor. Amazon built a whole principle around it called Invent and Simplify. Just keep asking, what's simpler and better for the person we're serving?
Musk and the rocket you don't throw away
Space felt impossible to simplify. Decades of contractors, cost-plus contracts, everyone saying that's just how much rockets cost. Musk asked a childlike question: what is a rocket actually made of, and what should that metal cost? Turns out the materials were a tiny slice of the price. So SpaceX started building more themselves, deleting parts instead of adding them, and chasing reusability. His mantra: "Simplicity is our mantra. It creates both reliability and low cost." And his test: "Try to delete the part or process." The tech was wildly complex. The idea was human: stop throwing the rocket away.

Complexity is unavoidable. Confusion isn't.

My 5-Step Script Before Big Meetings

I still do this. Out loud. In my car sometimes.

1. Name the decision

"The decision we need from you today is ___."

Say this first. It settles everyone, including you.

2. Force the one sentence

"___ is causing ___, and we're proposing ___."

No slides. Just say it. If you stumble, you aren't clear yet. That's okay, keep working it.

3. Make it about them

Ask yourself: so what does this mean for them?

Not "we optimized the ETL pipeline." More like "you'll have your numbers before your morning coffee instead of after lunch." People feel outcomes.

4. Honor the work, briefly

"We looked at three options. This was the hardest to build, but it's the simplest for you to use because ___." Or: "The easy version would have been ___. We didn't do that because ___."

This is the part most managers skip. Don't pretend it was easy. One honest line like this builds more credibility than showing all 20 subtasks ever did for me.

5. Invite the real question

"What part still feels fuzzy or risky to you?" Or: "Do you want the 30 second version or the 5 minute version?"

Don't ask "does that make sense?" You'll only get polite nods. When they start asking sharp questions, you did it right. That means they're with you.

Copy the Script: Fill This Out Before You Walk In

1. The decision we need from you today is ___.
2. ___ is causing ___, and we're proposing ___.
3. What this means for you: ___.
4. We looked at [number] options. This was the hardest to build, but it's the simplest for you to use because ___.
5. What part still feels fuzzy or risky to you? (Or: do you want the 30 second version or the 5 minute version?)

Quick Reminders
  • Complexity is unavoidable. Confusion isn't.
  • You can still carry every subtask in your back pocket. Just stop leading with them.
  • One honest line about what was hard beats a full spreadsheet of proof, every time.
  • If you stumble on the one-sentence version, you're not clear yet, that's information, not failure.
  • When the room starts asking sharp questions instead of nodding politely, you did it right.

I'm 15 years in and I still get nervous before the big ones. I still rehearse that one sentence. It's not a talent. It's just practice.
