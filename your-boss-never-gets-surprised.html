Your boss never gets surprised.

Everyone says managing up is a must-learn skill. It's too important to leave to chance. But almost nobody shows you how to actually do it. So here's how.

What this does: Your boss never gets surprised, never has to guess what you need, and starts describing you as "on top of it" and "easy to work with."
For: Managers who want to manage up without being political.
Time to implement: 15 minutes a day for 2 weeks.

The Core Idea

If everything sounds urgent, nothing is urgent.

You have to train your boss how to read you. Every update you send is one of two things:

  1. FYI, no action needed.
  2. I need something from you.

If you don't label it, your boss has to figure it out. And they will guess wrong.

This isn't about politics. A boss blindsided in a meeting doesn't just react to the problem. They quietly start reassessing whether they can trust your updates at all. That reassessment is quiet. It's expensive. And a two-minute heads-up prevents it completely.

The System: 3 Labels That Train Your Boss

Use the same three labels every time. Put the label at the start of the message.

01
FYI / No Action Needed
I got this. You don't need to do anything. This is context so you're not blindsided.
02
Input Needed
I have a plan, but I want your perspective before I act. The phrase that does the work: "Here's what I'm considering, but I want your read on it." It shows your boss you already did the first pass of thinking.
03
Decision / Help Needed
I need you to do something. I'm blocked without you.
Copy / Paste Scripts

Seven real situations. Grab the one you need right now.

When Bad News Is Coming Up in a Meeting, and You Haven't Said Anything Yet

FYI — No action needed: heads up before tomorrow's sync, the client is going to bring up a delay on their side of the project. Here's what happened and what I'm doing about it, so it doesn't catch you off guard in the room.

Why it works: your boss walks into that meeting already knowing, already has a response ready, and already trusts you're the kind of person who gives this warning instead of letting them find out live.

When You Have an Angry Customer, and You Have It Handled

FYI — No action needed: Acme is frustrated about the reporting issue. I talked to Sarah and Eng, we have a fix, and I'm replying now. I'll keep you posted if it escalates.

Why it works: you answered the four questions your boss would have had to ask. What happened? Is he aware? Does he have it? Will it escalate?

When a Metric Looks Bad

FYI — No action needed: SLA dropped from 96% to 87% this week. I pulled the data, driver is X. We've changed Y and are monitoring daily. Expect recovery next week. I'll flag if it doesn't.

Why it works: you didn't just report a problem. You found it, diagnosed it, and own the fix.

When You Made a Mistake

FYI — No action needed: flagging a mistake I made on the staffing report. It impacted headcount by 3. I've corrected it and added a check so it doesn't happen again.

Rule: tell them within 24 hours, before someone else does. No excuses.

When You Need Input Before You Act

Input needed: here's what I'm considering, but I want your read on it. Planning to respond to Acme with [this approach]. My take is [your take]. Any concerns before I send in the next hour?

Why it works: your boss knows this one requires a real response, not just a skim. You've already done the first pass of thinking, so all that's left is their read on it.

When You Need a Decision

Decision needed: we have two options for coverage next week. A costs $X and does Y, B costs $Z and does Q. I recommend A because [reason]. Can you confirm by 2pm so I can lock it?

Why it works: you already did the comparison. All that's left is a yes.

When Someone on Your Team Does Something Exceptional

FYI — For the leadership meeting: John stayed late 3 nights to get Acme back on track. Customer called him out by name. Big reason we retained them.

Why it works: you're helping your boss see people and outcomes they aren't close enough to see themselves.

Knowing When to Escalate

Not every problem fits in an FYI or a Decision ask. Some problems need to go up the chain entirely, and getting the timing wrong is its own kind of surprise.

Escalate too early and it reads as panic. Escalate too late and it reads like you were hiding something. Both mistakes come from the same root cause: not having a clear, honest test for when a problem is actually yours to solve.

The Escalation Test

Escalate when you're genuinely blocked and can't move forward without someone else's authority or resources, when the risk of staying quiet is bigger than the discomfort of speaking up, or when you've already tried the reasonable options and none of them worked. Don't escalate simply because a conversation is hard, or because you'd rather someone else deliver unwelcome news, or because you haven't actually tried to solve it yourself yet.

One direct question cuts through the noise in the moment: am I raising this because I've hit a real limit, or because I want someone else to handle something uncomfortable for me? Those two feel the same in the moment. They say very different things about you afterward.

What to Expect
Week 1
Your boss will still reply to your FYIs with questions. That's normal. They're learning to trust you.
Week 2
They stop treating your FYIs like problems. When they see "FYI, no action needed," they think "thanks" and move on. When they see "Input needed" or "Decision needed," they prioritize it. You trained them.
The 5-Minute Daily Check

Before you log off, ask three questions:

  1. Is there anything my boss would be uncomfortable learning from someone else first? If yes, send an FYI tonight.
  2. Is there anything I'm blocked on or need perspective on? If yes, send an Input or Decision request tonight.
  3. Is there a problem I've been quietly sitting on, hoping it resolves itself? Run it through the escalation test above before you decide.

Do that for 14 days.

Your New Standard

If it's important enough that you'd be uncomfortable if your boss learned about it from someone else first, tell them first.

Inform, Ask, or Go Over Their Head

Telling someone is not asking them, and this is where the three labels earn their keep. Informing your boss is an FYI — no action needed, you're just making sure they're not blindsided. Asking is Input Needed or Decision Needed — you need their read on it, or their actual sign-off, before you move. If you're not sure which one you're doing, that's usually the tell that it's the second one.

Example: Actually Informing

FYI — No action needed: moving our team's internal check-in from Tuesdays to Wednesdays next week, since it was conflicting with another recurring meeting. Nothing needed from you, just wanted you aware.

Example: Actually Asking

Decision needed: I'd like to move our external deadline with the client from Friday to the following Tuesday. Can I get your go-ahead before I communicate that to them?

If you're not sure whether something is your call, that uncertainty itself is the answer: ask.

The opposite mistake has a cost too — asking permission for decisions that were actually yours to make just signals you're not ready for more autonomy, no matter how good your judgment actually is.

Sometimes the right move isn't informing your boss or asking them at all. It's going over their head. That's allowed — the method is what makes or breaks it. Tell your boss first. That's what keeps the trust intact, before you ever raise something with their boss.

Example: Going Over Their Head

I want to be straightforward with you — I'm planning to raise the budget concern directly with Sarah, since it's ultimately her call and I don't think it's something you can resolve on your own. Wanted you to hear that from me first, not find out after.

Going over your boss's head rarely damages the relationship. Going over it without telling them almost always does.

One exception: if the concern is about your boss's own conduct, skip the heads-up and go straight to HR or their boss.
