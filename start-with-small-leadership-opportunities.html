Becoming a Manager · Article 2 of 8

Start with small leadership opportunities.

You do not need to lead a company-wide initiative to prove that you can lead. Most management careers begin with smaller opportunities that are easy to overlook.

Volunteer to coordinate a team meeting. Offer to organize a shared project plan. Take responsibility for documenting a process. Help your team prepare for an important presentation.

These tasks may not come with extra pay or a new title, but they give you opportunities to practice the work managers do every day. You will have to clarify expectations, organize information, communicate with different people and follow up when something is incomplete. Those are not administrative details separate from management. They are the foundation of management.

The goal is not to collect impressive-sounding assignments. The goal is to learn how to create progress through other people. That requires more than enthusiasm. It requires structure.

Before you take on a project, make sure everyone understands the goal, the deadline and their responsibilities. If the project is ambiguous, write down what you understand and ask the group to confirm it. This prevents people from working toward different definitions of success.

During the project, communicate regularly and address confusion early. Do not wait until the deadline is close to discover that two people made different assumptions. A short update can prevent a major delay.

When the project is finished, review what worked and what did not. Ask whether the timeline was realistic, whether responsibilities were clear and whether the communication process helped or created unnecessary work. This is how you turn a small assignment into evidence of leadership.

A small project can demonstrate that you know how to:

  • Define a goal
  • Break work into manageable steps
  • Assign ownership
  • Coordinate different priorities
  • Communicate expectations
  • Identify risks
  • Follow up without micromanaging
  • Resolve disagreements
  • Recognize contributions
  • Learn from the outcome

Do not wait for someone to hand you a perfect leadership opportunity. Look for friction in the normal flow of work. A recurring meeting with no clear outcome, a process that causes repeated errors, or a project with unclear ownership may be an opportunity to practice leadership. Start small enough that you can succeed, but choose work that involves other people.

Small Leadership Opportunities to Look For
  • Organizing a team meeting
  • Creating a project timeline
  • Coordinating a cross-functional task
  • Training a new employee
  • Documenting a recurring process
  • Leading a brainstorming session
  • Preparing a team presentation
  • Managing a shared deadline
  • Creating a checklist
  • Helping resolve a workflow problem
  • Coordinating a customer response
  • Organizing a retrospective after a project
  • Creating an onboarding guide
  • Facilitating a decision between competing options
  • Taking responsibility for a recurring team report
Project Kickoff Template

Use this when you are asked to coordinate a project.

Project goal: What are we trying to accomplish?
Business reason: Why does this matter, and what problem will it solve?
Deadline: When must the work be completed?
Responsibilities: Who owns each task?
Dependencies: What must happen before another task can begin?
Risks: What could delay the work or reduce its quality?
Communication plan: How often will we check in, and where will updates be shared?
Decision process: Who makes decisions if the group cannot agree?
Definition of success: How will we know the project worked?

After-Action Review Template

Ask these when the project ends.

What outcome did we achieve?
What worked well?
Where did confusion or delay occur?
What should we repeat?
What should we change?
What did I learn about coordinating people?
What recommendation should we carry into the next project?

Tip: Choose Projects With People Involved

A project that only requires you to work independently may demonstrate competence, but a project involving several people gives you more opportunities to demonstrate leadership. Look for assignments that require you to coordinate different priorities, clarify expectations, follow up, resolve confusion, share information, keep people accountable, make tradeoffs, communicate changes and help others succeed.

Do not volunteer for everything. Choose opportunities that are visible, useful and realistic alongside your existing responsibilities. A poorly managed extra assignment can hurt your reputation more than it helps.
